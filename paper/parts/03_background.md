# 2. Background

## 2.1 Flash floods and Boro in the haor

The haor basin is a low-lying wetland in north-eastern Bangladesh, where Boro is often the only crop of the year [@kamruzzaman2018]. Two hazards dominate: cold at the reproductive stage and flash flood at maturity [@rashid2018]. Floods are typically described as arriving in the first two weeks of April [@hossain2023], and the 2017 flood began on 27 March [@ahmed2017]. Timing is therefore central, because the same water can destroy a standing crop or arrive after the harvest. Evidence on these losses is mostly from single events or small areas: satellite mapping of the 2017 damage [@ahmed2017], household surveys [@dey2021; @ahmed2024] and local studies [@khan2012; @mondal2021]. The haor is also an adaptation setting, where short-duration varieties and earlier sowing are recommended [@hossain2023] and farmers report barriers to adapting [@baishakhy2023].

## 2.2 Floods and rice in Bangladesh

In Bangladesh, floods are not simply harmful. Districts more prone to flooding had higher cultivated area and productivity in 1978–2000, with yield falling in extreme floods and rising in normal floods and in the months after [@banerjee2010]. Boro production rose after the 1988 and 1998 floods, helped by residual water, while the Aman crop fell through both area and yield [@asada2005]. A district panel found meaningful flood effects on Boro production but no meaningful yield results [@hassan2019]. Studies of climate and rice mostly examine yield, using regions, stations or national series [@sarker2014; @hasan2016; @rahman2017]. One study covers both yield and cropping area, using national time series and climate variables [@amin2015]. Rainfall and self-reports are weak proxies for flood exposure in Bangladesh, which supports the use of satellite data [@guiteras2015].
