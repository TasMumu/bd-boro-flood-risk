# 1. Introduction

Assessing what a flood costs a farming region depends on what is measured. Most research on weather and crops has focused on yield [@cui2020; @wei2023], and yield is the natural outcome when a flood reduces grain per hectare. But a flood can also destroy a crop outright. If official yield is measured only on fields that were harvested, a destroyed field does not lower the yield estimate. It simply leaves the harvested area. Disaster assessments and research that look at yield alone could then miss the losses that matter most.

The evidence on this point is mixed, and the answer appears to depend on the setting. Globally, drought reduced cereal production through both area and yield, extreme heat mainly through yield, and no effect of floods could be identified in national data [@lesk2016]. A recent global study reports that submergence for more than a week reduces annual rice yield by 4.3 % [@li2025]. In the United States, harvested ratios fall under extreme heat [@cui2020], and shocks in planted and harvested area coincided with more than half of the production shocks across seven crops, although yield contributed more for some of them [@wei2023]. In Kansas wheat, however, losses came from yield and not from abandonment [@obembe2021]. For rice, typhoons reduced provincial production in a Philippine panel [@blanc2016].

Boro rice in Bangladesh is a useful test. It is the country's main rice crop, grown in the dry season and harvested from April to May. In the haor wetlands of the north-east, early flash floods can destroy the standing crop shortly before harvest [@kamruzzaman2018; @rashid2018], and the 2017 flood alone was reported to have cost 0.88 million tonnes of Boro [@hossain2023]. The Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) estimates yield as production per harvested hectare, from crop cuts on harvested fields [@bbs2026], so the reporting explanation can be tested directly: if destroyed fields leave no trace in yield, a flood should appear in area and production but not in yield.

We asked five questions. Q1: do flash floods reduce Boro production through harvested area rather than yield? Q2: do monsoon floods, which come before planting, act differently? Q3: are flood effects concentrated in the haor? Q4: does the vegetation index NDVI explain yield within a district? Q5: where is risk concentrated, and how stable is that ranking?

The analysis makes three contributions. It uses a validated panel of all 64 districts, with values printed by BBS and no modelled yields [@mumu2026a]. It checks official statistics against evidence independent of them, namely two flood records, a satellite measure of crop loss, and published damage figures. And its questions, models and pass rules were written down before the models were run, so that a weak result is reported as weak (Section 4.1 gives the limits of this claim). That turned out to matter: the area effect is clear for the 2017 flood, but the evidence for a general rule is only suggestive.
