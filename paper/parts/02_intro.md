# 1. Introduction

Assessing what a flood costs a farming region depends on what is measured. Most research on weather and crops has focused on yield [@cui2020; @wei2023], and yield is the natural outcome when a flood reduces grain per hectare. But a flood can also destroy a crop outright. If official yield is measured on crop that survives to be cut, a destroyed field does not lower the yield estimate, and the loss can appear only as lost area. Disaster assessments and research that look at yield alone could then miss the losses that matter most.

The evidence on this point is mixed, and the answer appears to depend on the setting. Globally, drought reduced cereal production through both area and yield, extreme heat mainly through yield, and no effect of floods could be identified in national data [@lesk2016]. A recent global study reports that submergence for more than a week reduces annual rice yield by 4.3 % [@li2025]. In the United States, harvested ratios fall under extreme heat [@cui2020], and shocks in planted and harvested area coincided with more than half of the production shocks across seven crops, although yield contributed more for some of them [@wei2023]. In Kansas wheat, however, losses came from yield and not from abandonment [@obembe2021]. For rice, typhoons reduced provincial production in a Philippine panel [@blanc2016].

Boro rice in Bangladesh is a useful test. It is the country's main rice crop, grown in the dry season and harvested from April to May. In the haor wetlands of the north-east, early flash floods can destroy the standing crop shortly before harvest [@kamruzzaman2018; @rashid2018], and the 2017 flood alone was reported to have cost 0.88 million tonnes of Boro [@hossain2023]. The Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) estimates yield from sample crop cuts, estimates crop area separately from sample clusters, and derives production by multiplying the two [@bbs2026]. The reporting explanation can therefore be tested: if destroyed fields leave the area estimate, a flood should appear in area and production but not in yield.

We asked five questions. Q1: do flash floods reduce Boro production through harvested area rather than yield? Q2: do monsoon floods, which come before planting, act differently? Q3: are flood effects concentrated in the haor? Q4: does the vegetation index NDVI explain yield within a district? Q5: where is risk concentrated, and how stable is that ranking?

The analysis makes three contributions. It uses a validated panel of all 64 districts, with values printed by BBS and no modelled yields [@mumu2026a]. It checks the official area series against other evidence, namely two flood records, a satellite measure of greenness loss, BBS's own damage reports and published damage figures. And it follows a written analysis plan with set pass rules, so that a weak result is reported as weak, although the plan was not preregistered (Section 4.1). That turned out to matter: the evidence is strong for the 2017 flood, but only suggestive for a general relationship.
